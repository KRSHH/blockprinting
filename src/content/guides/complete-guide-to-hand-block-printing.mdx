When I first arrived in **Bagru, Rajasthan** in 2010 on a Minerva Fellowship, I thought I understood what "handmade" meant. Then I spent my first week standing beside *Chhipa* printers in a courtyard 30 kilometers southwest of Jaipur, watching a single five-meter length of cotton pass through more than a dozen human hands before a single color finished developing.

Traditional hand block printing is not a nostalgic relic—it is an extraordinarily precise relief-printing system perfected over centuries of collaboration between woodcarvers, dyers, washers, and printers. Here is how the craft actually works on the workshop floor.

## The Block Family: Rekh, Datta, and Gadh

A classic multi-color hand block print is never made with a single stamp. Block carvers—often working in dedicated carving workshops in Jaipur or Farrukhabad—cut a matched set of blocks from seasoned **teak (*sagwan*)** or **sheesham**:

- **Rekh (Outline block):** Carries the fine linework that defines the motif and sets the repeat grid across the cloth.
- **Datta (Background block):** Fills the negative space around the design, often creating the deep earthy grounds Bagru is famous for.
- **Gadh (Filler block):** Drops interior colors into petals, leaves, and geometric centers.

Before a new block ever touches dye, printers soak or coat the carved face in mustard or sesame oil for several days. That simple step seals the timber against swelling and cracking through years of wet printing.

## Preparing the Cloth (*Saaj*) and Printing Table

Most beginners assume crisp printing comes down to how hard you press the block. In reality, half the battle is won before the first stamp lands:

1. **Scouring (*Saaj*):** Raw, loom-state cotton is full of starches, pectins, and natural waxes that repel color. Traditional workshops wash and soak cloth in a bath of fuller's earth (*multani mitti*), soda ash, and castor oil so the cellulose fibers open up cleanly.
2. **Tannin Pre-Treatment (*Harda*):** For natural mordant printing, scoured fabric is dipped in *harda* (myrobalan fruit powder), giving the cloth a warm, pale-ochre base that binds alum and iron permanently.
3. **Table Padding:** You cannot get a sharp impression on bare wood. Traditional long printing tables are layered with tightly stretched jute or wool felt beneath a clean cotton top sheet, giving just enough spring for every carved ridge to transfer evenly.

## Charging the Block and Printing by Eye

Next to the printer sits the ***kunda***—a wooden dye tray fitted with a bamboo lattice and several layers of wool felt soaked in thickened paste. The printer taps the block lightly onto the felt pad so only the raised relief picks up a thin, uniform film of color.

Starting from the left edge of the pinned cloth, the printer sets the ***rekh*** block straight down and strikes the carved wooden handle with the heel of the hand. Moving rhythmically down the table, the printer matches microscopic carved registration pins (*gudh*) along the edges of each repeat. Once the outline dries, the ***gadh*** and ***datta*** blocks follow in separate passes.

## Why Imperfection Is the Signature of the Hand

When I worked with independent fashion labels through **Studio Bagru**, one of the most important conversations we had with new designers was learning to read hand-printed cloth. Because every impression is inked and placed by hand, you will always spot subtle variations in ink density and microscopic meeting points where one block repeat joins the next.

In industrial rotary printing, those variations are eliminated. In authentic hand block printing, they are the proof that a living artisan stood at the table and built your textile one strike at a time.
