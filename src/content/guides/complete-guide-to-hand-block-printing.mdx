Hand block printing is one of the oldest and most tactile methods of ornamenting cloth. Before rotary screens or digital textile printers existed, master carvers and printers across India, Persia, East Asia, and Europe built intricate repeating patterns purely by hand using seasoned hardwood blocks and botanical dyes.

## Anatomy of a Block Printing Set

A single multi-color textile pattern rarely uses just one block. Traditional workshops carve a coordinated family of blocks from seasoned teak or sheesham:

- **Rekh (Outline block):** Carves the fine structural lines of the motif and establishes the grid of the repeat.
- **Datta (Background block):** Fills the negative space around the main motif.
- **Gadh (Filler block):** Stamps interior petals, leaves, and accent colors inside the outline.

Each block is seasoned in mustard or sesame oil for several days before its first use so the wood resists warping when exposed to moist dye pastes.

## Preparing the Cloth and Padded Table

Crisp impressions depend as much on the printing surface as on the carving itself.

1. **Scouring:** Boil or hot-wash natural cotton, linen, or khadi with soda ash (sodium carbonate) to strip industrial starch and natural waxes.
2. **Mordanting (for natural dyes):** Treat the scoured cloth with myrobalan (*harda*) tannin if you plan to print with alum or iron mordant pastes.
3. **Table padding:** A hard tabletop produces patchy prints. Stretch 3 to 5 layers of dense wool felt or jute matting over a flat wooden table, topped with a taut sheet of unbleached cotton.

## Stamping and Multi-Color Registration

Pin the scoured fabric smoothly across the padded table so it cannot shift mid-run. Charge the carved face of the block lightly on the felt-lined dye tray (*kunda*)—never submerge the block deep in liquid dye, or fine lines will blur.

Place the **rekh** block straight down onto the fabric and strike the handle firmly with the heel of your hand or a wooden mallet. Lift straight up to avoid smudging. Follow the tiny registration points carved into the block corners as you step across the cloth, then layer the **gadh** and **datta** blocks once the outline is touch-dry.

## Curing and Care of Printed Textiles

Allow freshly printed textiles to air-dry completely for 24 to 48 hours. Pigment prints are fixed with dry heat (a hot iron or curing press), while natural mordant prints proceed to the dye bath (such as madder or indigo) before a final soap boil that reveals their lasting brilliance.
