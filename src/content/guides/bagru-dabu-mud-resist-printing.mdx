Drive thirty kilometers southwest of Jaipur and the air changes. You start seeing lengths of indigo and madder cotton drying across flat rooftops and open courtyards, and you hear the steady, rhythmic thump of woodblocks hitting padded tables. That is **Bagru**—a town where the *Chhipa* community has practiced hand block printing for roughly four centuries.

Of all the techniques I learned during my years living in Bagru and building **Studio Bagru**, none captures the ingenuity of rural Indian textile craft quite like ***dabu***—mud-resist block printing.

## What Makes *Dabu* Work

Instead of stamping color onto cloth, *dabu* stamps a protective barrier **where you do not want dye to go**. The chemistry is deceptively humble, relying entirely on local earth and plant binders:

- **Black clay (*kali mitti*):** Dug from nearby seasonal ponds, providing the dense physical resist barrier.
- **Natural gum (*gond*):** Holds the clay together so it stamps cleanly without cracking off prematurely.
- **Slaked lime (*chuna*) and wheat chaff (*beedhan*):** Stabilize the paste and improve adhesion to cotton fibers.

Printers tread and strain this mixture until it reaches a smooth, velvety consistency. Any stray pebble or lump will ruin a fine woodblock impression.

## The Sawdust Trick and the Indigo Vat

Once the *dabu* paste is stamped onto the fabric, an assistant immediately scatters fine **sawdust (*burada*)** across the wet clay. The sawdust acts like blotting paper: it wicks surface moisture out of the mud, prevents neighboring folds from sticking together, and reinforces the resist crust.

After drying under the Rajasthan sun, the cloth goes straight into a sunken **indigo vat**. The printer lowers the fabric slowly and evenly so the clay doesn't wash away before the indigo bonds to the exposed fibers. For deep, midnight blues, the cloth is oxidized in the open air and dipped again—sometimes three or four times.

When the fabric is finally washed in clean water, the mud and sawdust dissolve back into the earth, revealing crisp cream or previously printed patterns floating against rich indigo. Layer a second round of *dabu* and a yellow pomegranate or turmeric over-dye, and you get classic **double-*dabu* (*do-dabu*)** textiles with teal, green, indigo, and ivory—all from a handful of natural ingredients.

## Bagru vs. Sanganer vs. Ajrakh

Designers sourcing in India often lump Rajasthan and Gujarat block printing together, but each cluster has a distinct DNA:

| Tradition | Region & Community | Signature Technique | Visual Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bagru** | Bagru, Rajasthan (*Chhipa*) | Direct natural printing & *dabu* mud-resist | Earthy *harda* grounds, indigo, madder red, iron black, bold *jal* florals & geometrics |
| **Sanganeri** | Sanganer, Jaipur (*Chhipa*) | Direct calico & *doo-rokhi* (double-sided) printing | Delicate botanicals, fine buds, and paisleys on bright white cloth |
| **Ajrakh** | Kutch, Gujarat (*Khatri*) | 14–16 step double-sided resist & mordant dyeing | Symmetrical geometric stars and architectural borders in deep indigo & crimson |

Understanding those regional differences isn't just academic—it helps designers match their artwork to the workshops whose generational skill sets fit the project best.
