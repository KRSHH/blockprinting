This is a blunt claim, and I stand behind it after fifteen years working alongside *Chhipa* printers in Bagru, Rajasthan: if you learn and respect these five rules, you will understand hand block printing more deeply than almost every fashion buyer, textile student, and retail brand selling "block-printed" cloth today.

All it takes is ten minutes. Five minutes to read these rules once, and five minutes to read them again.

1. **The print lives or dies before the block touches the cloth.** Raw cotton straight off the loom is coated in starch, pectin, and natural wax that repel dye. In Bagru, every meter of cloth goes through *saaj*—scouring in soda ash, fuller’s earth (*multani mitti*), and castor oil—followed by *harda* (myrobalan tannin) if natural mordants will be used. Skip scouring and your color washes down the drain.
2. **Never print on a hard surface.** A bare wooden table produces patchy, skeletal impressions because carved wood cannot conform to microscopic irregularities in woven yarn. Traditional printing tables (*patia*) are padded with four to five layers of dense wool felt or jute matting beneath a taut unbleached cotton cover. The pad gives just enough spring for every hairline ridge to transfer cleanly.
3. **Charge the block lightly on felt, never dip it in liquid.** Beside every printer sits the *kunda*—a wooden tray holding a bamboo lattice topped with felt soaked in thickened dye paste. Watery dye bleeds sideways by capillary action. Natural thickeners like guar gum, gum arabic, or tamarind seed powder turn liquid mordants into a honey-smooth paste so only the raised timber face picks up a thin, uniform film.
4. **Multi-color patterns require a family of carved blocks.** Serious textile printing uses matched sets carved from seasoned teak (*sagwan*) or sheesham: the *rekh* (outline block) sets the repeat grid, the *datta* fills background space, and the *gadh* drops interior accent colors. Tiny registration notches (*gudh*) carved into the block edges let the printer align each color by eye across five-meter lengths.
5. **Natural color bonds inside the fiber; pigment sits on top.** Synthetic pigments glue themselves to the surface of cloth with a plastic binder. Traditional botanical dyes—fermented iron-jaggery black (*syahi*), alum-mordanted madder root (*manjistha*), and fermented indigo (*neel*)—bond chemically inside the cotton yarn. That is why authentic hand block prints breathe against the skin and age gracefully over decades.
