A large percentage of garments marketed globally as "hand block printed in India" never touched a carved piece of teak. They were run on flatbed or rotary silkscreens—or printed digitally—using digitized copies of traditional Bagru and Sanganeri motifs.

When I founded Studio Bagru in 2016, teaching designers and buyers how to inspect cloth became half our job. You do not need a laboratory to spot authentic hand block printing. You need sixty seconds, good daylight, and these six forensic checks.

## Six Forensic Checks on Cloth

| Signal | Authentic Hand Block Print | Screen-Printed / Digital Imitation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Repeat joins** | Subtle hairline overlaps or tiny gaps every 15–25 cm where one wooden block meets the next | Continuous, mathematically invisible repeats across the entire bolt |
| **Color density** | Gentle tonal shifts between strikes as the printer re-inks on the *kunda* felt pad | Uniform, flat ink coverage from selvedge to selvedge |
| **Color registration** | Micro-shifts (0.5–1.5 mm) where *gadh* filler sits slightly off the *rekh* outline | Mechanical laser-tight alignment, or identical misregistration repeated endlessly |
| **Reverse side of cloth** | Natural dyes penetrate thin cotton (mulmul/voile) cleanly so the motif reads softly on the back | Digital prints and heavy acrylic pigments sit on top, leaving the back stark white |
| **Hand-feel** | Soft, breathable cotton; natural dye bonds inside the fiber | Stiff, slightly rubbery or chalky surface crust from acrylic binders |
| **Selvedge & motif scale** | Patterns built from compact 10–25 cm block dimensions | Oversized continuous gradients or photographic shading impossible on relief wood |

## 1. Hunt for the Block Join
Measure across the fabric with your eye. Traditional teakwood blocks rarely exceed 20–25 cm across because a heavier block exhausts a printer’s wrist over an eight-hour shift. Follow a repeating stem or border across one meter: on genuine hand-printed cloth, you will find quiet meeting points where one strike overlaps or kisses the next.

## 2. Check the Reverse Side
Flip the fabric over. Natural mordant dyes—madder, indigo, fermented iron black—wick into scoured cellulose yarn. On lightweight cotton like *mulmul* or cambric, the pattern bleeds softly through to the back. Digital inkjet prints on cotton usually leave the reverse side pale and blank.

## 3. Look for Non-Repeating Imperfections
Some commercial mills deliberately distress their digital artwork to fake a "handcrafted" look. Here is the giveaway: **a printed fake repeats the exact same flaw every 64 centimeters.** Real human variation never repeats identically twice.

## 4. Smell and Touch the Textile
Freshly washed natural-dyed Bagru cloth carries a faint earthy scent of *harda*, clay, or indigo and feels completely supple. Heavy synthetic pigment pastes leave a detectable plastic film over solid dark areas.

## 5. Ask Who Carved the Blocks
Real workshops can name their craft cluster (Bagru, Sanganer, Ajrakhpur, Farrukhabad) and show the carved teakwood blocks used for your print. If a supplier claims "hand block printed" at factory screen-print prices with a five-day turnaround during monsoon season, you are looking at a screen print.
